Japanese Curry — Rich, Mild and Comforting

How to make Japanese curry at home: roux blocks, vegetables, protein, and serving it properly with rice.

What Japanese curry is

Japanese curry is a thick, mild, slightly sweet stew, very different from Indian or Thai curry. It is made with a roux of flour, fat, and curry powder, or more commonly with ready-made curry roux blocks, and it is served over rice with pickles. It is one of Japan's most popular home dishes, eaten weekly in many households, and it is a favourite with children because it is gentle rather than spicy. The flavour is rich and savoury with a background sweetness from onions and carrots, and the texture is smooth and thick enough to coat the rice.

Using roux blocks

Curry roux blocks are the easiest way to make Japanese curry, and they are what most Japanese home cooks use. They are sold in boxes of cubes, in mild, medium, and hot strengths. Brown your meat and vegetables, add water, simmer until tender, then turn off the heat and stir in the roux cubes until they dissolve. Simmer gently for five more minutes until the sauce thickens. Do not boil hard after adding the roux, because it can catch on the bottom. One box usually serves four to six people, and the strength on the packet is a guide, not a rule.

The vegetables and protein

The classic combination is onion, carrot, and potato, cut into large chunks, with chicken, pork, or beef. Cook the onion first until soft and sweet, then add the meat to brown, then the carrot and potato. Some cooks add a grated apple or a spoonful of honey for sweetness, and a little tomato ketchup or Worcestershire sauce for depth. These additions are traditional and worth trying. Simmer until the potato is tender but not falling apart, about twenty minutes. The vegetables should hold their shape in the thick sauce.

Making curry from scratch

If you cannot find roux blocks, make a roux: melt butter, stir in flour, and cook it until it smells nutty, then add curry powder and a little garam masala. Add stock gradually, then simmer with the vegetables and meat as above. From-scratch curry takes longer but lets you control the spice and sweetness. A spoonful of chocolate or a little instant coffee is a traditional secret ingredient that deepens the flavour without making the curry taste of chocolate. Start with small amounts and taste as you go.

Serving

Serve Japanese curry in a shallow bowl or on a plate, with a mound of steamed rice on one side and the curry ladled beside it, not over the top. Add fukujinzuke, the sweet pickled vegetables that traditionally accompany curry, or any pickle you have. A small salad adds freshness. The curry should be thick enough to sit on the plate without running. Leftover curry tastes even better the next day, because the flavours deepen overnight, and it keeps in the fridge for three days or freezes well for a month.

Variations

Once you can make the basic curry, try variations: katsu curry with a breaded pork cutlet on top, curry udon where the sauce is thinned and served over noodles, or a vegetarian curry with aubergine, mushrooms, and chickpeas. Curry bread, a deep-fried bun filled with curry, is a popular bakery item. The roux method is flexible, so the same base works for many dishes. Japanese curry is forgiving, filling, and hard to get wrong, which is why it is a staple in so many homes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Japanese curry spicy?

No, it is mild and slightly sweet. Roux blocks come in mild, medium, and hot, but even the hot version is gentler than most Indian or Thai curries. Add chilli if you want more heat.

Can I freeze Japanese curry?

Yes. Cool it completely, freeze it in portions for up to a month, and reheat gently. The potatoes may soften slightly, but the flavour improves after a day.

What is fukujinzuke?

Fukujinzuke is a sweet pickled vegetable relish, usually made from daikon, aubergine, and lotus root, traditionally served with Japanese curry. Any sweet pickle works as a substitute.
